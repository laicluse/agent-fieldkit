# Unslop

Slop is defaultism: convergence on the mode. A model asked for a number says 7; asked for a page it gives the ground, the type pairing, the corner radius and the hover that occurred most often in its training. Each value is defensible alone. Together they are the aesthetic average, and to a practised eye the average reads as beginner's work.

Unslop replaces those values with decisions a craftsman would make for this surface. It covers appearance, colour, shape, on-screen wording, behaviour and repeated patterns. It leaves alone what the product does: features, information order, structure and data are the owner's, and stay as they are. Wording, feedback and transitions may change; their meaning, the states, the conditions and the steps of a task may not. Where design reasoning advises changing the order or the copy of a generic page, this boundary takes precedence. Moving to the second most likely value is not unslop; neither is adding things on top of what is there.

Apply [design reasoning](../eye-of-the-beholder/design-reasoning.md) throughout. A request that names unslop or slop delegates the visual decisions: decide yourself, build it, show it. An invocation without a pointer means the surface the conversation or the working directory is about. Recall and record [the user's taste](../eye-of-the-beholder/taste.md) as it describes, and let a [judge](../review/judge.md) see the result before the user does, sized as that file describes.

## Procedure

1. **Capture what is there.** Render the surface with realistic data and keep the capture.
2. **Inventory the mode.** List every visible decision with its current value, layer by layer: ground and palette, type, shape (radius, border, shadow, containers inside containers), spacing and density, labels and on-screen wording, icons, behaviour (hover, transition, feedback, confirmation). Mark each value you would have produced for any subject. Mark separately every value that repeats across elements with different roles: one radius, one border, one transition everywhere is the clearest trace of the mode. Include what you added yourself in this session.
3. **Derive the style from this product, with evidence.** A style picked by association (a children's feast, so primary colours) is the mode one step further, and so is the look you would give any surface once asked to unslop it. Write that look down first; the result may not match it. Then write the product's story in a few lines: who uses it, for which occasion, and which objects, names, dates and habits are in it. Collect real material: what the owner supplied or owns first, then real work you open and look at in this session, kept as captures. A style recalled from memory has not been looked at. Record the direction in the project's visual-language record before any style, different from the latest entries in `surfaces.md` of the [reference library](../explore/reference-library.md), and give every colour, typeface and form its source: sampled from which capture, following from which fact of the story, or chosen by the user. A value without a source is a default. Swap the subject as a test: reasoning that holds as well for another product has not used this one. A source makes a value specific, not good: a typeface or colour with a story can still be the wrong one for its place.
4. **Set the level from current work.** Your memory of what good design looks like is dated and averaged, so the level cannot come from it either. Open and capture current work of this kind that practised designers made this year or last, and write down the mechanisms that make it read as theirs: the type scale and pairing, the rhythm of space, density, how colour is rationed, how numbers align, how states and motion are treated. Prefer work whose design date you can establish and record an unknown date as unknown; a recent capture does not prove a recent design. Add what you learn to the reference library, and write the mechanisms and every choice they change into the project's record. Where no capture can be made or kept, say so and report the level as unverified. The product's material decides what is specific; these mechanisms decide the level. A result that is specific but below that level is themed, not designed.
5. **Decide each marked value by role.** Read the foundation of each plane you decide on ([typography](../eye-of-the-beholder/foundations/typography.md), [colour](../eye-of-the-beholder/foundations/colour.md), [space](../eye-of-the-beholder/foundations/space.md), [motion](../eye-of-the-beholder/foundations/motion.md), [icons](../eye-of-the-beholder/foundations/icons.md), [wording](../eye-of-the-beholder/foundations/wording.md)); a value follows its rules or breaks one on purpose. For each value ask what a practised designer would do for this element on this surface. Fitness for the role outranks the source: reading text, data and controls take a face made for reading, and a display face from the material goes where display type belongs. What the user chose or supplied stays in the design; where it is applied still follows the role. A value follows from the role of its element: a status block, a text row, a field and a toggle do not share a shape because they share a page. Remove decoration that serves no role. Keep what the user chose or supplied, and keep conventional controls conventional.
6. **Build the styles from an empty file.** Editing the old stylesheet toward the new one keeps its values. Where styles live in utility classes, component variants or shared tokens, restate every value there instead of adjusting some. Change markup only where a decided form needs it; features, order and data stay.
7. **Compare renders.** Place the new render beside the capture from step 1, beside the current work from step 4 at the same scale, and against the latest surfaces in the log, which holds descriptions; captures stay in their projects. Go through the inventory: a marked value that survived needs its reason written down. When a deterministic slop detector is available, run it; a clean result is a floor, not proof. Check the narrow width and the states the surface has.
8. **Show and record.** Show old and new side by side, add the surface to `surfaces.md`, and leave the verdict on beauty to the user. Say plainly where the result falls short of the level from step 4. A rejected direction becomes a constraint for a new style in step 3, not a list of adjustments.

## Limits

Accessibility, legibility and the real task outrank novelty. Unslop establishes that each visible value was decided for this surface; whether the result is good is the user's judgement of the render.
